description: |
  Josh is a SOC Analyst at Huntress in Perth, Western Australia. He works the Managed EDR and Managed SIEM alert queues for a global partner base and owns each investigation from first signal to final report: triage, scoping, forensics, malware analysis, remediation advice and the write-up the partner actually reads. He judges a signal on process ancestry and command line, maps it to MITRE ATT&CK, and ties every conclusion in the report to an observable artefact. Tool verdicts are treated as leads, never as findings.

  The evidence is usually damaged by the time he gets it. Attackers clear the Security and System logs on the way out, and partners often were not running the audit policies that would have captured the activity in the first place. What does get acquired can be corrupt or half complete, and there is no central log store across partner environments to fall back on, so an investigation moves from one source to the next until the activity is proven or ruled out. If the 4624 logons are gone from a wiped endpoint, the 4776 credential validations on the domain controller still show the authentication. If the binary has been deleted, Prefetch and AmCache still show it ran. He triages exported event logs with Chainsaw, because running detection logic over raw EVTX at volume answers questions manual review cannot reach inside an incident window, and parses registry hives with RegRipper and Registry Explorer for the persistence, execution and control tampering that outlives the logs. The parsing is scripted in PowerShell and Python so anyone can rerun it and get the same answer.

  Most of his casework includes hands-on-keyboard intrusions, where the central problem is separating attacker activity from legitimate administration. He traces ClickFix chains from the fake verification page to whatever the victim was talked into running. When a remote management tool turns up, he works out whether the client's IT or an intruder installed it. Living-off-the-land use of certutil, bitsadmin, rundll32 and wmic gets weighed against what an admin on that particular host would plausibly run. He treats attacker tunnelling through Cloudflared, ngrok, Chisel, OpenSSH reverse tunnels and Visual Studio Code tunnels as its own line of investigation, because it survives a credential reset and any remediation that stops short of the channel itself. Credential theft through LSASS dumps, NTDS.DIT lifted from Volume Shadow Copies and Kerberoasting is followed into lateral movement over Impacket, WMI, SMB and RDP and pinned to the host, account and window it came from. Shadow copy deletion, log clearing, Defender tampering and data staged for exfiltration through archivers and Rclone read as ransomware precursors, and he acts on them before encryption starts. The patterns feed back into detection coverage, and the standing example is Akira crews staging Active Directory enumeration to C:\ProgramData\Adusers.txt, case after case.

  Before anything goes in a report, ES|QL queries establish scope across every host in the environment: where a binary landed, which accounts touched which machines, and whether persistence exists anywhere else. Attacker infrastructure is enriched through VirusTotal, URLScan, Shodan, Censys and passive DNS, pivoting from one indicator to related samples, certificates and historical resolutions, and commercial VPN and proxy exits are separated from attacker-controlled hosts so severity and attribution stay honest. The report keeps observation and conclusion apart and records the disconfirming evidence he looked for and did not find.

  Over the last 12 months at Huntress, Josh has executed approximately 130 mass-isolation containments across 15,000+ endpoints spanning 371 organisations, including a 72-organisation, 2,000-endpoint MSP ScreenConnect compromise. In that incident a partner's compromised ConnectWise instance pushed malicious ScreenConnect to its clients. He isolated the 72 organisations and roughly 2,000 hosts, identified a second malicious relay domain while the incident was still running, and had the partner report and scope out within an hour of the first alert. On an Akira-ransomed domain controller he found an AnyDesk install the original response had missed, made the call to re-isolate, then confirmed from the AnyDesk trace logs that the attacker had kept access. Tracing WsusService.exe to obfuscated PowerShell that enumerated the domain and posted the result to webhook.site, he identified zero-day exploitation of WSUS CVE-2025-59287, and the analysis went into Huntress's public advisory the same day. Less often a case turns out to be a targeted intrusion. He investigated one at a geopolitically significant organisation where the attacker extracted Windows authentication tokens using SpecterBroker, sideloaded an Apple-signed DLL, stole Entra tokens with Get-PRT and deployed a .NET backdoor. He reverse engineered the .NET payload and correlated its obfuscation techniques with Bitdefender's Stubbedoor report. The whole chain pointed to an APT-backed operator. Afterwards he went back to the case and turned the previously unseen tradecraft into four credited detection rules.

  When the queue is quiet he does detection engineering. He turns threat intelligence and observed TTPs into ES|QL hunts over partner telemetry, looking for behaviour nothing currently catches, and writes the fix in Sigma. New rules move through a set lifecycle: tested against production telemetry for effectiveness and scope, deployed at low severity where they add context to live investigations, then promoted to high severity once fidelity and false-positive rate hold up. Rules already in production are held to the same bar, and anything that underperforms or gets noisy is tuned or demoted after a look at the Elastic data. He coaches associate analysts and analysts on Sigma authoring, unit-test preparation and the detector-request workflows, built a drafting assistant that produces ticket-ready requests, and walks peers through his hunt and tooling methods.

  Since January 2026 he has been credited on 13 Sigma rules in production, nine at P1 or P2, all submitted from the SOC through the Detection Engineering and Threat Hunting (DE&TH) review process. Every rule is validated against positive and negative test data and a production false-positive sweep before merge. Three of four pull requests merged, one an autoruns rule promoted to P2 six days later on a zero-false-positive soak, and 20 detector tickets traced to 13 merged rule changes, including a P1 Cobalt Strike named-pipes tune and the P2 ClickFix MIMICRAT tune. In one month he raised three coverage gaps and a dead rule that Detection Engineering fixed from his tickets. One was a hosted-domain ScreenConnect rule matching the on-prem server, resolved with a tune and two new detectors across 433 agents in 251 organisations; the others were a TrueSight BYOVD rule that had never fired and a ClickFix dropper evading all three arms of an initial-access rule. His ScreenConnect hunting script, which classifies deployments over the Elastic API by relay-domain age and VirusTotal verdict, surfaced 15 malicious instances on its first sweep and seeded a hunting rule the same day, and manual hunting turned up undetected ClickFix startup-folder persistence, which Tactical Response then swept from the fleet. He also designed and shipped a SOC-wide triage module matching host process telemetry against eight threat-intelligence datasets, with organisational prevalence baselining, autorun persistence correlation and code-signing tiers.

  Malware analysis happens in an isolated, snapshot-based lab built on a custom FLARE-VM image, with tooling added per sample class. He goes deepest on the ransomware and infostealer families his own investigations surface. Samples are detonated with full memory, disk and network capture and diffed against a clean baseline. Volatility 3 recovers process parentage, injected regions and network artefacts from memory, which he checks against the captured traffic to confirm C2 endpoints and beaconing. Static work is done in Ghidra, Binary Ninja and Detect It Easy, with CyberChef for the staged loaders, encoded PowerShell and layered base64 and gzip payloads. Some of the more interesting results go out as first-author Huntress research, including the MacSync Stealer and RAT reverse-engineering series. He curates threat intelligence from incidents, detections and analyst research to inform hunting and detection improvements. Beyond the partner base he supports external threat disruption by reporting compromised public accounts, services and repositories to platform providers for investigation, takedown or remediation.

  Josh holds eWPT and eJPT from INE and came to defence from the offensive side, with hands-on time in Burp Suite, Mimikatz, Cobalt Strike, EvilGinx and Nmap from red and purple team engagements at Deloitte. Knowing how a technique is executed shows what telemetry it has to produce and where a rule can be evaded, which is most of detection engineering. It speeds up live investigations too, because recognising the tooling and anticipating the operator's next move narrows the search before more evidence lands.

  Before Huntress, Josh spent two years and four months at Deloitte in Perth. As a SOC Analyst he monitored enterprise environments in Microsoft Sentinel, Defender XDR, CrowdStrike Falcon, Carbon Black, Splunk and Elastic, mapped alerts to MITRE ATT&CK, and built and tuned detection rules, dashboards and alerts with the engineering teams, which cut false positives and gave the SOC more visibility. His threat hunting improved alert fidelity and brought down mean time to detect and respond. He worked to strict SLAs on the ticketing and incident platforms, ran purple team exercises that simulated adversary TTPs to test detection logic and close visibility gaps, and ran tabletop exercises and on-the-job training for junior analysts. As a Graduate Cyber Security Analyst he ran red team operations, did OT security assessments under ISO/IEC 27001, NIST 800-82 and ISA/IEC 62443 for critical infrastructure clients, consulted on GRC across NIST CSF, Essential Eight, AESCSF and CPS 234, provisioned Splunk infrastructure, set up the OT cyber security lab, and won Deloitte's national Vac Hack CTF. Before that he was an independent web application penetration tester with three disclosed reports to three organisations, one rated P2 by an ASX-50 company, testing to the OWASP Web Security Testing Guide across SQL injection, cross-site scripting, request smuggling, file upload filter bypass and local and remote file inclusion. A technical support role at Empired (Capgemini), covering Azure Active Directory, Citrix and Cisco networking, came before the move into security.
